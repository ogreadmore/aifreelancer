02 / The present answer

## The American story is clearer. The older connection is still missing.

No parent, immigrant generation, overseas home, or surname-transition event has yet been established for Aaron. No single origin theory currently deserves to be called probable.

The strongest progress is a firmer framework for testing those questions. Contemporary records identify Aaron’s wife, stated residences, land transactions and estate. Separately, original court testimony clarifies a proposed James–David Oliphant family. DNA comparisons now distinguish promising unresolved matches from matches whose shared paternal ancestry is much older.

Recorded

### Aaron’s wife and Georgia household have a solid anchor.

A deed dated 29 January 1821 calls Aaron of Hancock County, Georgia, and identifies his wife Rebecca as a daughter of deceased Solomon Rainwater. It names *her* father, not Aaron’s. His estate administration establishes that he was dead by 14 December 1826. [[1]](./index.html#s1) [[4]](./index.html#s4)

Recorded

### The James–David candidate family is more distinct.

George Gordon explicitly acknowledged that he was deceased James Oliphant’s son-in-law. David Oliphant was named as a coadministrator in Charleston. Neither observation identifies Aaron as a relative; David is not thereby proved to be James’s son or the painter. [[6]](./index.html#s6)

Unresolved

### Devaney is a useful comparison, not a recovered former surname.

A relatively close Y67 Devaney match and a separate R-A823 DeVenney tester justify finer comparison. They are two different people, and neither result yet establishes a recent common paternal ancestor with this Oliphant line. [[8]](./index.html#s8)

### Which candidates deserve the next look?

**These are research priorities, not odds of a former surname.** Devaney leads the named DNA comparisons, Wood follows, and seven broad-only Y67 matches remain tied. None is a probable identification of Aaron’s paternal family. The James–David records form a separate investigative track.

Dates below belong to documents. A purchase does not necessarily establish residence, and people with the same name are not automatically the same person.

### An estate sale and debt proceedings

An Aaron bought goods at Solomon Rainwater’s December 1814 estate sales. Original registers name Aaron in debt proceedings on 26 December 1816 and 18 March 1817. The family context makes the earlier identity plausible; the sale alone proves neither residence nor kinship. [[2]](./index.html#s2)

The Pendleton deed identifies Aaron of Hancock County and his wife Rebecca, Solomon Rainwater’s daughter. This is the earliest secure Georgia residence statement found in this investigation. [[1]](./index.html#s1)

On 25 December 1823, Aaron of Greene County bought 245¾ acres on Shoulderbone Creek. 04 / The genetic evidence

## A paternal branch is a clue. It is not an address or a surname.

Y-DNA follows the direct paternal line. Autosomal DNA reflects many ancestral lines. These answer different questions, and a shared surname in an autosomal match’s tree does not identify the source of the shared DNA. [[8]](./index.html#s8)

R-A823

23

Y67 matches reviewed

9

Still too broadly typed for equivalent exclusion

Fourteen of the reviewed Y67 comparators have more-resolved results that place their split on older branches, within the limits described in the private audit. Nine remain insufficiently resolved. This narrows particular comparisons; it cannot exclude every family bearing a surname. [[8]](./index.html#s8)

### Why Devaney stays on the list

One Y67 match is at an official genetic distance of five but is displayed only as broad R-M269. A separate DeVenney tester has R-A823. The needed next step is a finer SNP comparison: do either share a younger branch or reliable private variants with this line?

### Why confidence remains limited

Another five-step Y67 match splits much further back on the paternal tree. Similar STR distance can therefore conceal very different relationships. Shared R-A823, surname spelling counts and reported Irish origins cannot by themselves identify Aaron’s father.

Autosomal comparisons include service-confirmed triangulations, but none has been assigned to Aaron or a unique ancestral couple. Paternal cousins share ancestry through both paternal grandparents. Several surname spellings or related matches may therefore reflect the same connection—or a different branch entirely.

### The missing historical anchor

An independently documented direct-male descendant through another son of Aaron would help test whether this Y line reaches Aaron. A collateral through another son of Solomon would anchor it no earlier than Solomon. A single descendant’s result cannot independently verify every historical father–son link.

05 / Competing explanations

## What we think—and what could change it.

Confidence describes support for a proposition, not the likelihood that a useful record exists. These are overlapping possibilities, not mutually exclusive choices or calculated probabilities. [[12]](./index.html#s12)

A

### Aaron was American-born; the immigration was earlier.

Moderate working preference

**Why it leads:** the South Carolina record context and Solomon’s later paternal-birthplace statement give affirmative, if qualified, support. This is stronger than reasoning from a missing passenger record.

**What is missing:** a contemporary birth, parentage or childhood record. Earlier residence in America does not establish an American birth.

Most useful test → Direct-Aaron military, land-entry, church or earlier household evidence.

B

### The deeper paternal family had Irish, possibly Ulster, roots.

Broad direction only

**Why it matters:** relevant A823 comparison labels and the unresolved Devaney match make Ireland a reasonable genetic research direction. Support is low to moderate for the broad direction, low for a specific locality or route.

**What is missing:** independently documented comparison pedigrees and a sufficiently recent paternal connection. Self-reported tester origins and uneven testing cannot locate the immigrant. Scotland remains open.

Most useful test → A younger shared SNP branch paired with independently traced paternal pedigrees.

C

### A Devaney-related male line eventually used Oliphant.

Low historical confidence

**Why it stays active:** two distinct genetic comparators and documentary or autosomal naming clues make this the leading named family comparison.

**What is missing:** a recent shared paternal branch, a record naming Aaron’s parents, or any documented surname transition. High research priority is not high confidence in the explanation.

Most useful test → Resolve the broad Y67 match and separately compare the A823 tester at mutually readable DNA positions.

D

### Aaron connects to the Charleston–Union James and David family.

Low confidence in the link

**Why it stays active:** this is a genuine American Oliphant family with better-documented relationships, land and possible Scottish connections. It broadly fits the remembered tentative James–David suggestion.

**What is missing:** the Aaron bridge. The Union James, dead by January 1793, must remain distinct from a Laurens James alive in October 1797. The painter David and physician David must also remain distinct. The administrator’s identity is still open.

Most useful test → A contemporary record connecting Aaron to that family; the requested Scottish documents may clarify the candidates first.

### The alternative we must keep testing

The Devaney Y connection may be old, while any autosomal Devaney ancestry comes through another branch. An unidentified or untested family may explain the paternal line. Eliminating some named testers cannot prove whichever surname remains.

No positive evidence currently establishes a Jamaica route or places a name change, adoption or mistaken father specifically at Aaron. The lack of an identified birth record is a gap, not proof of any of those events.
